# Prioritizing Under Product Ambiguity

## Private scenario card

Rahul is a fictional India-based candidate in a remote behavioral round. The interview uses three different stories and probes exact contribution, fair representation of disagreement, evidence, failure, and changed behavior. The candidate should sound prepared but not recite perfectly shaped STAR monologues.

## Dialogue

### Connection, introduction, and frame — approximately 00:00-03:00

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Hi, Rahul. Can you hear me clearly?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** Hi, Tessa. Yes, I can hear you. Is my audio okay?

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** It is. There was a brief delay when you joined, but it seems stable now. If it changes, we'll pause rather than talk over the connection.

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** Sounds good. Thank you.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** We have about thirty-five minutes. I'll ask for a few examples from your experience and follow up on details. We don't need confidential names or exact customer data. Please focus on what you personally did and learned.

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** Yes, that works for me.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Before we get into the main round, could you give me a brief description of yourself and what you've been working on recently?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I'm an engineer with experience in analytics dashboards in React. My current scope is hands-on delivery plus ownership of bounded cross-team changes. I have removed confidential names and customer details from the examples, but I can still explain the decisions, mistakes, and outcomes at the level I owned them.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What is the clearest example of a decision that was yours rather than the team's?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I asked product to define the decision a user should make, reviewed session recordings, and proposed a freshness indicator and saved view before new visualization work. I built the indicator behind a flag and added an event for successful return visits. That is the clearest recent example because I can separate my judgment from the team's execution and explain what I got wrong.

### Behavioral scenario 1 — approximately 03:00-11:00

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Tell me about a time the problem statement was ambiguous.

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I was asked to make a customer dashboard more engaging without a defined behavior or metric. The request came from a quarterly goal after usage declined. I initially assumed we needed more charts, but support notes suggested users could not tell which data was stale. My role was not to solve every part alone. I asked product to define the decision a user should make, reviewed session recordings, and proposed a freshness indicator and saved view before new visualization work. I built the indicator behind a flag and added an event for successful return visits. Users with the indicator reopened the dashboard more often and support questions about stale data fell. We did not claim the feature caused every improvement because the sample was limited.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What exactly was your decision rather than the team's?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** My decision was the specific action and boundary in the story: I asked product to define the decision a user should make, reviewed session recordings, and proposed a freshness indicator and saved view before new visualization work. I built the indicator behind a flag and added an event for successful return visits. I consulted the relevant owner, but I was accountable for making the recommendation, making the risk visible, and following through. Where another team implemented part of it, I would describe that as their work rather than absorb it into 'I.'

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What was the other person's or team's reasonable position?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** Their position came from a real constraint in the context: The request came from a quarterly goal after usage declined. I initially assumed we needed more charts, but support notes suggested users could not tell which data was stale. I may have disagreed with the first solution, but I did not assume the stakeholder was careless. Making that constraint explicit helped us compare outcomes instead of arguing labels.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What did you get wrong?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I spent two days sketching new charts before clarifying the outcome. That was low-cost, but it showed my instinct to convert ambiguity into implementation too quickly. That is the part I would preserve in the answer even though it makes the story less polished. The result improved only after I changed my own approach; it was not a story where everyone else needed to adapt.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did you know the result was better?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** Users with the indicator reopened the dashboard more often and support questions about stale data fell. We did not claim the feature caused every improvement because the sample was limited. I would use those bounded operational and stakeholder signals rather than invent a precise percentage that was never measured. Where causality was uncertain, I would say so.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did you communicate while the outcome was uncertain?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I stated what was known, what was still a hypothesis, the immediate containment or reversible step, the owner, and the next update time. For remote teammates I put that in the shared record instead of relying on a call summary. I avoided promising resolution before the evidence supported it.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Did anyone disagree with your final approach?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** Yes, at least one stakeholder preferred the faster or more familiar path. I restated their concern, showed the tradeoff, and made the decision owner explicit. After the decision, I recorded what evidence would cause us to revisit it so disagreement did not continue as informal resistance.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What did you do in the first hour or day, before the full solution was known?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I separated containment from diagnosis. The immediate step protected the user or preserved evidence while remaining reversible; then I assigned the next investigation and communication checkpoint. The relevant context was that the request came from a quarterly goal after usage declined. I initially assumed we needed more charts, but support notes suggested users could not tell which data was stale. I did not wait for a perfect root cause before stating the impact, and I did not let urgency justify an irreversible shortcut.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What evidence could have shown that your decision was wrong?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I named a measurable or observable condition tied to the result: users with the indicator reopened the dashboard more often and support questions about stale data fell. We did not claim the feature caused every improvement because the sample was limited. If user harm, error rate, adoption, or operational cost moved the wrong way, we would pause or reopen the decision. That made the proposal falsifiable. I would not use stakeholder agreement alone as proof that the technical or customer outcome was correct.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did your level affect what you owned and what you escalated?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I owned the bounded engineering decision, evidence, and follow-through within my role. I escalated policy, financial, security, or organization-wide authority to the named owner with a recommendation rather than simply handing off the problem. I did not claim authority I lacked, and I did not use that limit to avoid doing the analysis needed for a good decision.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What would you do differently now, and what changed in your later behavior?

**Rahul Das — Candidate:** I now write a one-sentence user outcome and the riskiest unknown before opening the codebase. I would act earlier at the decision point and keep the successful mechanism, not simply say I would communicate more.
