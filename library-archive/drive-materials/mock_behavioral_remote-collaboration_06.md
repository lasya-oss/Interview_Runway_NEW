# Remote Collaboration Across Three Time Zones

## Private scenario card

Pooja is a fictional India-based candidate in a remote behavioral round. The interview uses three different stories and probes exact contribution, fair representation of disagreement, evidence, failure, and changed behavior. The candidate should sound prepared but not recite perfectly shaped STAR monologues.

## Dialogue

### Connection, introduction, and frame — approximately 00:00-03:00

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Hi, Pooja. Can you hear me clearly?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** Hi, Tessa. Yes, I can hear you. Is my audio okay?

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** It is. There was a brief delay when you joined, but it seems stable now. If it changes, we'll pause rather than talk over the connection.

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** Sounds good. Thank you.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** We have about thirty-five minutes. I'll ask for a few examples from your experience and follow up on details. We don't need confidential names or exact customer data. Please focus on what you personally did and learned.

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** Yes, that works for me.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Before we get into the main round, could you give me a brief description of yourself and what you've been working on recently?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I'm an engineer with experience in API gateways and schema migration across India, Germany, and California. My current scope is hands-on delivery plus ownership of bounded cross-team changes. I have removed confidential names and customer details from the examples, but I can still explain the decisions, mistakes, and outcomes at the level I owned them.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What is the clearest example of a decision that was yours rather than the team's?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I created a migration ledger with owner, compatibility result, decision needed, and deadline. Each region recorded a five-minute handoff and updated one source document. We reserved overlap only for rejected exceptions. That is the clearest recent example because I can separate my judgment from the team's execution and explain what I got wrong.

### Behavioral scenario 1 — approximately 03:00-11:00

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Tell me about collaborating across several time zones.

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I coordinated a gateway-schema migration across Kochi, Berlin, and California with only ninety minutes of common overlap. The old route configuration allowed ambiguous defaults. Forty services needed migration, and the US security team owned one policy decision. My role was not to solve every part alone. I created a migration ledger with owner, compatibility result, decision needed, and deadline. Each region recorded a five-minute handoff and updated one source document. We reserved overlap only for rejected exceptions. Thirty-eight services migrated on schedule; two received explicit extensions. No region waited more than one workday for a routine decision.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What exactly was your decision rather than the team's?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** My decision was the specific action and boundary in the story: I created a migration ledger with owner, compatibility result, decision needed, and deadline. Each region recorded a five-minute handoff and updated one source document. We reserved overlap only for rejected exceptions. I consulted the relevant owner, but I was accountable for making the recommendation, making the risk visible, and following through. Where another team implemented part of it, I would describe that as their work rather than absorb it into 'I.'

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What was the other person's or team's reasonable position?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** Their position came from a real constraint in the context: The old route configuration allowed ambiguous defaults. Forty services needed migration, and the US security team owned one policy decision. I may have disagreed with the first solution, but I did not assume the stakeholder was careless. Making that constraint explicit helped us compare outcomes instead of arguing labels.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What did you get wrong?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** My first handoffs were chronological logs. Teammates could not see the requested decision, so I changed them to decision-first summaries. That is the part I would preserve in the answer even though it makes the story less polished. The result improved only after I changed my own approach; it was not a story where everyone else needed to adapt.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did you know the result was better?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** Thirty-eight services migrated on schedule; two received explicit extensions. No region waited more than one workday for a routine decision. I would use those bounded operational and stakeholder signals rather than invent a precise percentage that was never measured. Where causality was uncertain, I would say so.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did you communicate while the outcome was uncertain?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I stated what was known, what was still a hypothesis, the immediate containment or reversible step, the owner, and the next update time. For remote teammates I put that in the shared record instead of relying on a call summary. I avoided promising resolution before the evidence supported it.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Did anyone disagree with your final approach?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** Yes, at least one stakeholder preferred the faster or more familiar path. I restated their concern, showed the tradeoff, and made the decision owner explicit. After the decision, I recorded what evidence would cause us to revisit it so disagreement did not continue as informal resistance.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What did you do in the first hour or day, before the full solution was known?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I separated containment from diagnosis. The immediate step protected the user or preserved evidence while remaining reversible; then I assigned the next investigation and communication checkpoint. The relevant context was that the old route configuration allowed ambiguous defaults. Forty services needed migration, and the US security team owned one policy decision. I did not wait for a perfect root cause before stating the impact, and I did not let urgency justify an irreversible shortcut.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What evidence could have shown that your decision was wrong?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I named a measurable or observable condition tied to the result: thirty-eight services migrated on schedule; two received explicit extensions. No region waited more than one workday for a routine decision. If user harm, error rate, adoption, or operational cost moved the wrong way, we would pause or reopen the decision. That made the proposal falsifiable. I would not use stakeholder agreement alone as proof that the technical or customer outcome was correct.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did your level affect what you owned and what you escalated?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I owned the bounded engineering decision, evidence, and follow-through within my role. I escalated policy, financial, security, or organization-wide authority to the named owner with a recommendation rather than simply handing off the problem. I did not claim authority I lacked, and I did not use that limit to avoid doing the analysis needed for a good decision.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Would you make the same decision now?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I would preserve the central choice if the same evidence held, but I would change the process at the weak point: my first handoffs were chronological logs. Teammates could not see the requested decision, so I changed them to decision-first summaries. The later mechanism is more important than saying I would act faster. My updated approach is this: I now design the information handoff with the same care as the technical interface. If the constraints changed, I would re-evaluate rather than treat the original outcome as a reusable slogan.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did you avoid overclaiming the result?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I separated the immediate operational or stakeholder evidence from broader causality. The observed result was that thirty-eight services migrated on schedule; two received explicit extensions. No region waited more than one workday for a routine decision. I did not convert that into a company-wide percentage or claim that my action alone caused every change. I also named the remaining limitation. That makes the story less dramatic but gives the interviewer evidence they can trust.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What would you do differently now, and what changed in your later behavior?

**Pooja Menon — Candidate:** I now design the information handoff with the same care as the technical interface. I would act earlier at the decision point and keep the successful mechanism, not simply say I would communicate more.
