# Leadership Without a Manager Title

## Private scenario card

Sneha is a fictional India-based candidate in a remote behavioral round. The interview uses three different stories and probes exact contribution, fair representation of disagreement, evidence, failure, and changed behavior. The candidate should sound prepared but not recite perfectly shaped STAR monologues.

## Dialogue

### Connection, introduction, and frame — approximately 00:00-03:00

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Hi, Sneha. Can you hear me clearly?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** Hi, Tessa. Yes, I can hear you. Is my audio okay?

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** It is. There was a brief delay when you joined, but it seems stable now. If it changes, we'll pause rather than talk over the connection.

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** Sounds good. Thank you.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** We have about thirty-five minutes. I'll ask for a few examples from your experience and follow up on details. We don't need confidential names or exact customer data. Please focus on what you personally did and learned.

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** Yes, that works for me.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Before we get into the main round, could you give me a brief description of yourself and what you've been working on recently?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I'm an engineer with experience in shared data contracts and lakehouse governance. My current scope is hands-on delivery plus ownership of bounded cross-team changes. I have removed confidential names and customer details from the examples, but I can still explain the decisions, mistakes, and outcomes at the level I owned them.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What is the clearest example of a decision that was yours rather than the team's?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I quantified incidents, interviewed two resistant teams, built a compatibility check in their existing CI, and recruited one respected producer team as a pilot. I created an exception path with expiry rather than claiming every migration could be immediate. That is the clearest recent example because I can separate my judgment from the team's execution and explain what I got wrong.

### Behavioral scenario 1 — approximately 03:00-11:00

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Tell me about influencing without formal authority.

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I led adoption of versioned event contracts across six teams without owning those teams. Schema changes repeatedly broke analytics, but producers saw a central registry as process overhead. My platform team could not mandate their release workflow. My role was not to solve every part alone. I quantified incidents, interviewed two resistant teams, built a compatibility check in their existing CI, and recruited one respected producer team as a pilot. I created an exception path with expiry rather than claiming every migration could be immediate. Five teams adopted within two quarters and breaking changes reaching curated data fell materially. One legacy team remained on the exception path with a dated plan.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What exactly was your decision rather than the team's?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** My decision was the specific action and boundary in the story: I quantified incidents, interviewed two resistant teams, built a compatibility check in their existing CI, and recruited one respected producer team as a pilot. I created an exception path with expiry rather than claiming every migration could be immediate. I consulted the relevant owner, but I was accountable for making the recommendation, making the risk visible, and following through. Where another team implemented part of it, I would describe that as their work rather than absorb it into 'I.'

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What was the other person's or team's reasonable position?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** Their position came from a real constraint in the context: Schema changes repeatedly broke analytics, but producers saw a central registry as process overhead. My platform team could not mandate their release workflow. I may have disagreed with the first solution, but I did not assume the stakeholder was careless. Making that constraint explicit helped us compare outcomes instead of arguing labels.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What did you get wrong?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** My first proposal required every producer to adopt the platform team's terminology and created unnecessary resistance. That is the part I would preserve in the answer even though it makes the story less polished. The result improved only after I changed my own approach; it was not a story where everyone else needed to adapt.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did you know the result was better?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** Five teams adopted within two quarters and breaking changes reaching curated data fell materially. One legacy team remained on the exception path with a dated plan. I would use those bounded operational and stakeholder signals rather than invent a precise percentage that was never measured. Where causality was uncertain, I would say so.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did you communicate while the outcome was uncertain?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I stated what was known, what was still a hypothesis, the immediate containment or reversible step, the owner, and the next update time. For remote teammates I put that in the shared record instead of relying on a call summary. I avoided promising resolution before the evidence supported it.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Did anyone disagree with your final approach?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** Yes, at least one stakeholder preferred the faster or more familiar path. I restated their concern, showed the tradeoff, and made the decision owner explicit. After the decision, I recorded what evidence would cause us to revisit it so disagreement did not continue as informal resistance.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What did you do in the first hour or day, before the full solution was known?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I separated containment from diagnosis. The immediate step protected the user or preserved evidence while remaining reversible; then I assigned the next investigation and communication checkpoint. The relevant context was that schema changes repeatedly broke analytics, but producers saw a central registry as process overhead. My platform team could not mandate their release workflow. I did not wait for a perfect root cause before stating the impact, and I did not let urgency justify an irreversible shortcut.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What evidence could have shown that your decision was wrong?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I named a measurable or observable condition tied to the result: five teams adopted within two quarters and breaking changes reaching curated data fell materially. One legacy team remained on the exception path with a dated plan. If user harm, error rate, adoption, or operational cost moved the wrong way, we would pause or reopen the decision. That made the proposal falsifiable. I would not use stakeholder agreement alone as proof that the technical or customer outcome was correct.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did your level affect what you owned and what you escalated?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I owned the bounded engineering decision, evidence, and follow-through within my role. I escalated policy, financial, security, or organization-wide authority to the named owner with a recommendation rather than simply handing off the problem. I did not claim authority I lacked, and I did not use that limit to avoid doing the analysis needed for a good decision.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** Would you make the same decision now?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I would preserve the central choice if the same evidence held, but I would change the process at the weak point: my first proposal required every producer to adopt the platform team's terminology and created unnecessary resistance. The later mechanism is more important than saying I would act faster. My updated approach is this: I now design adoption around the user's workflow and use mandates only when risk and authority actually support them. If the constraints changed, I would re-evaluate rather than treat the original outcome as a reusable slogan.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** How did you avoid overclaiming the result?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I separated the immediate operational or stakeholder evidence from broader causality. The observed result was that five teams adopted within two quarters and breaking changes reaching curated data fell materially. One legacy team remained on the exception path with a dated plan. I did not convert that into a company-wide percentage or claim that my action alone caused every change. I also named the remaining limitation. That makes the story less dramatic but gives the interviewer evidence they can trust.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What tradeoff did you knowingly accept?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** The chosen action improved the most important user, reliability, or delivery outcome but introduced cost in time, complexity, or local flexibility. I named that cost, assigned an owner, and set a condition for revisiting it. A credible decision is rarely free. If I cannot explain who absorbed the cost, I probably have not described the tradeoff completely.

**Tessa Morgan — Interviewer:** What would you do differently now, and what changed in your later behavior?

**Sneha Reddy — Candidate:** I now design adoption around the user's workflow and use mandates only when risk and authority actually support them. I would act earlier at the decision point and keep the successful mechanism, not simply say I would communicate more.
